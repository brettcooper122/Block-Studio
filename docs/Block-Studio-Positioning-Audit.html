LANGUAGE AUDIT / 001 — PREPARED FOR BLOCK STUDIO

How the best AI-native studios talk about what they do

A teardown of the language, hooks, and structure the sharpest small product studios use, and a plain map of what to borrow, what to skip, and how to say what Block Studio already does without it reading as a stretch.

STUDIED: Lazarev · 925studios · Ramotion · Spaceberry · Webgamma · Tennis · Phoenux · The Gradient

What they're actually selling

None of these studios sell "design." They each pick one sharp idea and repeat it everywhere. Here's the through-line behind each one.

The one move they all make

Every studio here replaces the word "design" with a business outcome. Design is the method; the promise is adoption, conversion, trust, funding, or speed. The single most quoted line from the whole audit says it bluntly:

"If design isn't moving revenue, adoption, or retention, it's decoration."
— Lazarev
This is the entire mindset shift in one sentence. They're not promising pretty screens, they're promising the thing the buyer's boss actually asks them about.

The pattern worth stealing

Notice none of these are "we do UX/UI for everyone." Each is a narrow, opinionated claim, often defined as much by what they refuse as what they offer. Phoenux literally lists "Not for 'guarantee #1 in ChatGPT' SEO theatre." Spaceberry says "zero tolerance for mediocre." Saying no loudly is itself the positioning.

The exact language they use

Real pulled lines, grouped by the job each one does. This is the vocabulary that was missing, not because the skill wasn't there, but because nobody had translated it into the buyer's terms yet.

Hook type 1 — Name the buyer's pain, not your service

"From stalled pilot to a launch people adopt."
— Lazarev
Names a specific, humbling problem (a pilot that went nowhere) and the relief. No mention of "design" at all.
"Users aren't converting, retention is lower than it should be, or the product works but doesn't feel good enough to compete."
— Spaceberry
They describe the client's symptoms so precisely the client thinks "that's exactly me." Diagnosis sells before the cure does.

Hook type 2 — The philosophy line (a stated belief)

"AI is probabilistic. Dynamic. Alive. You can't design it in static mocks. You have to build it to design it."
— The Gradient
This is Block Studio's positioning, already written by someone else. It justifies building in real code instead of handing off Figma, which is exactly the working method already in place.
"A designer who sits in Figma all day won't cut it."
— Spaceberry
Draws a line between old-way designers and new-way ones, and puts themselves on the right side. Block Studio is on the right side of this line too.

Hook type 3 — Reframe the risk of NOT hiring them

"...reduce the risk that your next AI release is another unused toggle in a settings menu."
— Lazarev
Makes the cost of inaction vivid and specific. Fear of a wasted release does the selling.

Tone — how they sound

Confident, plain, and specific. Short declarative sentences. Almost no adjectives about themselves ("beautiful," "stunning"). The confidence comes from naming the buyer's world precisely, not from praising their own work. Spaceberry even writes in a slightly funny voice ("Don't be like Dwight Schrute"). The takeaway: sounding credible does not require sounding corporate, it requires sounding specific.

What's on their sites — and what's actually needed

These sites carry dozens of pages built by full teams. Block Studio is one person. Here's the honest split between what's worth copying now and what's overkill at this stage.

Build these now

  • One sharp hero line (pain + outcome, not "product designer")
  • 3–4 named services, described by outcome
  • 2–3 case studies with a before/after and one number
  • A short "who this is for / not for" section
  • One clear next step (book a call)
  • A simple About that tells the real story

Skip for now (overkill at 1 person)

  • 40+ SEO service pages (Lazarev's footer) — that's a full-time team's job
  • Subscription pricing tiers & billing infrastructure
  • A blog committed to weekly — one good post beats ten stubs
  • Awards walls (those come later)
  • Multi-office location lists
  • Motion-heavy interactions that take weeks to build

The clever features worth noting

"Still unsure? Let ChatGPT or Claude explore Spaceberry for you."
— Spaceberry (buttons that open an AI pre-filled with a prompt about them)
Cheap to build, memorable, and perfectly on-brand for an AI-native studio. This is the kind of single clever touch worth one afternoon — spend the boldness in one place.
The 3-day / 8-hour free trial
— Spaceberry & 925studios (risk reversal)
Removes the buyer's fear of committing to an unknown. A scoped "paid pilot" or "first deliverable in X days" is the solo-friendly version.
The "not for you if…" section
— Lazarev & Phoenux
Counterintuitively builds trust. Turning away wrong-fit work signals real confidence about who the work is right for. Easy to write, high impact.

The three services, written in Block Studio's voice

Each one framed by the pain it resolves, the buyer it speaks to, and copy grounded in the exact language the sharpest studios in this lane are already using.

Service 01 — Agentic End-to-End UX Design & Development

The pain it resolves. Product teams building AI-adjacent experiences are stuck in a handoff loop built for a slower era. A designer ships Figma, engineering waits on tickets, and users get something four sprints later that half-answers the original brief. Once the product involves an AI agent, the loop compounds; static mocks cannot capture behaviour that is dynamic, probabilistic, and shifting by the day.

What clients actually get. Design and build, in the same set of hands, from the first sketch through to production-ready code. States, confidence displays, and handoff patterns for when the agent needs to pass control back to a person, all built in real code so the experience can be tested before the team commits to it.

Most designers hand off files and wait weeks for engineering to bring them to life. We bring designs to life ourselves, in real code, so the product you test with users is the product your team can ship.
Draft copy — Agentic End-to-End UX Design & Development
↪️ Static mocks cannot capture AI behaviour. Agentic UX has to be built to be designed; anything less is a guess dressed up in Figma.

Service 02 — Creative Strategy: user & market research

The pain it resolves. Founders fall in love with a solution before confirming enough people have the problem, and have it badly enough to pay to solve it. The validation itself often carries a built-in trap: leading questions asked of socially connected respondents, feedback that confirms what the team already wants to hear. The result is a product built on flattering data, launched into a market that never asked for it.

What clients actually get. User interviews scoped to the exact question the business needs answered, competitive analysis that filters out the fluff and surfaces the patterns worth borrowing, and positioning work that connects the product to the language the market is already using in searches, forum posts, and reviews. Research that shapes the decision, not research that decorates one already made.

Most teams design for the customer they imagine, then wonder why the real one never shows up. Creative strategy connects your product to the language the market is already using: the words your buyer types into a search bar, not the words your team writes on a roadmap.
Draft copy — Creative Strategy
↪️ Research should shape the decision, not decorate one already made. From the outset, this is about finding the pattern the market is showing you, so you build the product it is asking for.
Alternative hook

Service 03 — Rapid Pattern Generation & System Scaling

The pain it resolves. A design system exists, and it worked well for the first eighteen months. Now the product has grown past what the system was scoped for; new patterns are needed weekly, and the team is either rebuilding the system from scratch or watching hardcoded values quietly slip into production. Neither is sustainable. Neither is necessary.

What clients actually get. New components and patterns generated to slot into the existing system, respecting token hierarchy from primitive to semantic to component. Governance stays intact; the library keeps its integrity as it scales. This is the work agents cannot do alone, and juniors take too long to do carefully. It sits in the middle, and that middle is where the value is.

Your design system does not need a rebuild. It needs someone who can extend it at the pace your product is actually shipping, without breaking the hierarchy it was built on.
Draft copy — Rapid Pattern Generation & System Scaling
↪️ Systems drift when growth outpaces governance. New patterns generated in the shape of your existing tokens, carrying the same rules, so scaling does not cost you the consistency the system was built for in the first place.
Alternative hook

How the three connect

Each service resolves a different failure mode, but they share a common thread: the work happens closer to the product than most design engagements permit. Creative Strategy answers what to build. Agentic End-to-End brings the answer to life in real code. Rapid Pattern Generation keeps the system holding as the product scales past its original brief. Together they cover the arc from the question to the shipped feature, without the handoff gaps that eat the weeks of runway a founder does not have.

The part that's actually in the way

The feeling going into this audit was that these sites talk about business and value in a way that felt out of reach, and that it made the work feel like a stretch. Worth taking that apart directly, since it was the real blocker, not the skill.

The gap is a vocabulary gap, not a capability gap.

Read back through the Hooks tab. Every line that stood out is describing something already being done, just described in the buyer's language instead of a designer's. "From stalled pilot to a launch people adopt" is not a harder skill than the enterprise work already done on those accounts. It's the same work, named differently.

These studios didn't out-design anyone. They out-described the work. And description is learnable in an afternoon, which is what this document is for.

The myths, and what's actually true

MYTH: "They're bigger and more capable."
925studios is essentially one person (Mohammad Yusuf, Top-Rated on Upwork). Spaceberry says outright it would "rather have ten designers than fifty." Their websites look like big teams; several are as small as Block Studio or close to it. The polish is copywriting and a good template, not headcount.
MYTH: "Business and conversion language would never come naturally."
The same week this audit was built also involved pricing, positioning, conversion, retainers, and funnel decisions for Block Studio itself. That thinking was already happening; it just hadn't been seen mirrored back in polished web copy yet. Now the vocabulary bank sits on the Hooks tab.
MYTH: "A team of one is a weakness to hide."
Ramotion sells "fewer projects, more attention." Spaceberry sells "sharpest, not biggest." Solo and senior is a positioning, not a limitation: the client works directly with the person doing the work, no juniors, no handoff telephone. Worth naming as the advantage it is.
MYTH: "The 40-page site, the awards, the subscription tiers are all needed."
No. See the Structure tab. Those are the artifacts of years of operation, not the price of entry. A one-person site with a sharp hero, three outcome-named services, two real case studies, and a booking link competes fine. More would actually read as less honest at this stage.

The one thing to hold onto

Feeling like an imposter usually means comparing your own behind-the-scenes to someone else's finished website. The doubts and unfinished pages are visible from the inside; only the published surface is visible from the outside. That's not a fair comparison, and it isn't evidence about ability. The work was real. The clients were real. The skill was real. What was missing was the language, and that's now sitting in the other tabs of this document.
