# Block Studio Positioning Audit — Handoff

Companion file: `Block-Studio-Positioning-Audit.html` (the interactive document itself, ready to open in a browser or hand to Claude Code).

## Why this project started

The starting point was a feeling, not a task. While reading a stack of competitor studio websites, Brett noticed they spoke about business outcomes, conversion, and customer value in a register that felt foreign, and it produced a sharp imposter feeling: "I am a team of one, and I would never think to write like this." The brief was to study those sites properly, learn how they build that language, and use it to close the gap for Block Studio (Brett's freelance product design and strategy practice, run solo).

## The core finding

None of these studios sell "design." Each one swaps the word for a business outcome: adoption, conversion, retention, trust, funding, or speed. Design is positioned as the method, never the pitch. The line that captures it best:

> "If design isn't moving revenue, adoption, or retention, it's decoration." — Lazarev

## Each studio's core bet

| Studio | The idea they own | Who it's for |
|---|---|---|
| Lazarev | Design that moves revenue and adoption for complex AI and data products | Heads of AI, B2B founders under pressure to show usage |
| The Gradient | "You can't design AI in static mocks, you have to build it to design it" | Teams building real AI products, not chat skins |
| Spaceberry | "We're not building the biggest studio. We're building the sharpest one." | Post-seed startups needing senior design without a full hire |
| 925studios | Investor-grade product design at startup speed | AI SaaS and fintech founders who need to move fast |
| Ramotion | Takes fewer projects on purpose, depth over volume | High-growth startups wanting senior attention |
| Phoenux | Named AI-native service lines plus a clear list of work they refuse | Product teams adding AI without breaking trust |
| Webgamma | Every screen treated as a conversion step in a funnel | Products with users but weak activation or retention |

The pattern worth stealing: none of them claim to do "UX/UI for everyone." Each makes a narrow, opinionated claim, often defined as much by what it refuses as by what it offers. Phoenux states outright it is "not for guarantee-number-one-in-ChatGPT SEO theatre." Spaceberry states "zero tolerance for mediocre." Saying no loudly is itself the positioning.

## The language they actually use

Three recurring hook types, with real pulled examples:

**Name the buyer's pain, not your service**
> "From stalled pilot to a launch people adopt." — Lazarev
> "Users aren't converting, retention is lower than it should be, or the product works but doesn't feel good enough to compete." — Spaceberry

**State a belief (the philosophy line)**
> "AI is probabilistic. Dynamic. Alive. You can't design it in static mocks. You have to build it to design it." — The Gradient
> "A designer who sits in Figma all day won't cut it." — Spaceberry

**Reframe the cost of not hiring them**
> "...reduce the risk that your next AI release is another unused toggle in a settings menu." — Lazarev

**Keyword bank** that recurs across all of them, and that a buyer in this category expects to hear: adoption, conversion, retention, trust, activation, ship/shipped, end-to-end, production-ready, design system, rapid prototyping, validate, zero-to-one, explainability, agentic, investor-grade, sprint pace, complex-to-clear, business outcome.

**Tone**: confident, plain, specific. Short declarative sentences, almost no self-praise ("beautiful," "stunning"). Confidence comes from naming the buyer's world precisely rather than complimenting their own work. The takeaway is that Block Studio does not need to sound corporate to sound credible, it needs to sound specific.

## Site structure — what to build and what to skip

**Worth building at this stage**: one sharp hero line built from pain plus outcome, three to four named services described by outcome, two to three case studies with a before/after and a real number, a short "who this is for and who it isn't" section, one clear call to action, and an honest About section.

**Overkill for a team of one right now**: dozens of programmatic SEO service pages (Lazarev runs this because it has a dedicated SEO team; it does not translate to a solo practice), subscription pricing tiers and billing infrastructure, a blog cadence that demands weekly output, awards walls, multi-office listings, and heavy motion work that eats weeks to build.

Three specific features worth noting: Spaceberry's buttons that open a pre-filled ChatGPT or Claude prompt about the studio (cheap, memorable, on-brand for an AI-native shop), the three-day or eight-hour free trial that Spaceberry and 925studios both use as risk reversal, and the "not for you if..." section that both Lazarev and Phoenux run, which builds trust by turning away the wrong-fit work.

## Industries Block Studio can credibly claim

**Strongest claim, lead with these**: enterprise and B2B product (from the Mitsubishi, Hyundai, and Kraft Heinz work), AI-native products (the actual working method of building with AI tools is the credential here), and design systems (the OpenText Blueprint work is enterprise-grade system work already).

**Credible with the right framing, once a case study exists**: fintech, loyalty and rewards (Scene+), marketplaces (Kijiji), automotive (Hyundai, Mitsubishi), SaaS and B2B tools, and accessibility-critical products.

Across every studio audited, the verticals that show up most often, meaning where the budget and demand sit, are AI products, fintech, healthtech, B2B SaaS, and web3. Block Studio has a genuine claim to the first four. The recommendation was to lead with one or two rather than naming all of them at once.

## The positioning formula

> For **[specific buyer]** who **[specific pain]**, we **[outcome in their words]**, by **[unfair advantage]**, not **[the old, slow way]**.

The interactive document includes a tap-through builder using this formula, plus three starter hero line directions:

> "We turn ideas into working products, designed and built end-to-end, in weeks rather than quarters."
>
> "Most designers hand off a Figma file. We hand off something your users can actually click."
>
> "One senior team, no agency overhead, no junior handoff, no gap between the design and what ships."

## Reframing the imposter feeling

This was addressed directly because it was doing more damage than any real positioning gap:

- **"They're bigger and more capable than me."** 925studios is essentially one person. Spaceberry says outright it would rather have ten designers than fifty. The polish on these sites comes from copywriting and a good template, not headcount.
- **"I'd never think in terms of business and conversion."** The tax, pricing, positioning, and retainer work done in this same stretch of conversations already proves otherwise. The vocabulary was the only thing missing, and it is now captured above.
- **"A team of one is a weakness to hide."** Ramotion sells "fewer projects, more attention." Spaceberry sells "sharpest, not biggest." Solo and senior is a position, not a limitation, and the document recommends naming it as the advantage it is.
- **"I need the forty-page site, the awards, the pricing tiers."** No. A one-person site with a sharp hero, three outcome-named services, two real case studies, and a booking link competes fine. More would read as less honest at this stage.

The closing note in the document: feeling like an imposter usually means comparing your own behind-the-scenes to someone else's finished website. The work, the clients, and the skill were already real. What was missing was the language.

## Where this fed into Block Studio's actual service offering

The audit was not left as theory. It shaped three real service lines, written in Block Studio's voice (first person plural, Canadian spelling, grounded in the studios researched above):

**Service 01 — Agentic End-to-End UX Design & Development.** Resolves the handoff loop where a designer ships Figma, engineering waits on tickets, and users get something four sprints later that half answers the brief, a problem that compounds once an AI agent is involved because static mocks cannot capture behaviour that shifts day to day. Clients get design and build in the same hands, from first sketch through to production-ready code, including states, confidence displays, and handoff patterns for when the agent needs to return control to a person.

**Service 02 — Creative Strategy (user and market research).** Resolves the trap of founders falling for a solution before confirming the problem is real and painful enough to pay for, especially when validation comes from leading questions asked of friendly respondents. Clients get scoped user interviews, competitive analysis that filters out the fluff, and positioning grounded in the language the market already uses in search, forums, and reviews.

**Service 03 — Rapid Pattern Generation & System Scaling.** Resolves the moment a design system has outgrown its original scope and the team is stuck choosing between a full rebuild or letting hardcoded values slip into production. Clients get new components generated to slot into the existing system, respecting the token hierarchy from primitive to semantic to component, so governance and consistency survive the scaling.

How the three connect: Creative Strategy answers what to build, Agentic End-to-End brings the answer to life in real code, and Rapid Pattern Generation keeps the system holding as the product outgrows its original brief. Together they cover the distance from question to shipped feature without the handoff gaps that eat the weeks a founder does not have to spare.

The coaching offer was flagged as something to soft launch rather than build a full funnel around immediately, given how much was already running in parallel.
